## The problem

Offshore risers fatigue under combined wave loading and vessel motion. Predicting the loading environment well enough to make fatigue and motion calls in near-real-time is a model-engineering problem as much as a physics one. The simulators that produce ground truth are too slow to call inside an operational loop.

The brief: train ML surrogates on simulated sea-state data so significant wave height, fatigue contributors, and wave heading can be predicted from cheaper inputs at inference time, with full retrain-to-predict automation around them.

## Feature engineering

156 features per sample, drawn from physical primaries (peak period, spectral moments), derived statistics, and circular-aware encodings for the heading variable (sin/cos pairs plus distance-to-nearest-bin features). Adaptive scaling per family: standard for spectra, robust for tail-heavy distributions, no scaling for already-bounded categoricals.

## Hierarchical heading model

Predicting wave heading directly with regression collapses on the wrap point. A 359-degree prediction for a 1-degree truth is a 2-degree error, not 358. The fix was a two-stage model: a coarse classifier picks a heading bin (north, south, east, west), then a fine regressor predicts the offset within that bin. The composed model beats either head alone by a wide margin.

## Wave height

Random Forest won on the significant wave height head after a sweep over depth, estimators, and feature-subset rules. XGBoost and an MLP came within a hair of the same accuracy. The choice was made on training cost and inspectability, not raw accuracy. Domain reviewers want to see what the model looked at, and a Random Forest gives that back for free.

## Retrain to predict

New simulations land in object storage. A job re-derives features, triggers an evaluation sweep, and if a candidate beats the live model on the held-out test, it gets promoted. Strict campaign-level dataset partitioning: a simulation campaign goes entirely into either train or test, never both.

## What broke

The first heading regressor looked spectacular on a stratified random split and fell apart on a temporal split. The model was learning per-burst structure that did not generalise across simulation campaigns. Switching to campaign-level splits cost a few R-squared points but produced numbers that survived contact with new data. That was the highest-impact change in the whole project.
