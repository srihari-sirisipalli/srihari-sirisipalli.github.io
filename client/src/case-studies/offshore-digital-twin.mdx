## The problem

Offshore risers fatigue under combined wave loading and vessel motion. Predicting the loading environment well enough to make fatigue and motion calls in near-real-time is a model-engineering problem as much as a physics one. The simulators that produce ground truth are too slow to call inside an operational loop. The brief was: train ML surrogates on simulated sea-state data so that significant wave height, fatigue contributors, and circular wave heading can be predicted from cheaper inputs at inference time, with full retrain-to-predict automation around them.

## Constraints

- **No leakage**: 88,560 simulated sea states is enough that careless splits will look great in CV and explode in production.
- **Circular targets**: wave heading is angular. Standard MAE on degrees gets the wrap-around case wrong; a 359° prediction for a 1° truth is a 2° error, not 358°.
- **Retrain on demand**: the models have to be cheap enough to re-fit when new simulations land, with provenance trackable end-to-end.
- **Hierarchy matters**: classification + regression composed correctly outperforms either head alone.

## Approach

**Feature engineering.** 156 features per sample, including physical primaries (peak period, spectral moments), derived statistics, and **circular-aware encodings** for the heading variable (sin/cos pair plus distance-to-nearest-bin features). Adaptive scaling per feature family: standard for spectra, robust for tail-heavy distributions, no scaling for already-bounded categoricals.

**Hierarchical heading model.** Predicting wave heading directly with regression collapses on the wrap point. The fix was a two-stage model: a coarse classifier picks a heading bin (north / south / east / west / etc.), then a fine regressor predicts the offset within that bin. Combined, worst-case error dropped 41%; max error landed at 12.5°.

**Wave height.** The R² = 0.9992 result on significant wave height came from a Random Forest after a careful sweep over depth, n-estimators, and feature-subset rules. Worth noting that XGBoost and the MLP were both within ~0.001 R²; the choice was made on training cost and inspectability, not raw accuracy.

**Experiment scale.** 3,525 distinct ML/DL experiments × hyperparameter sweeps = approximately 1.08M model fits across the project. Tracking provenance (which simulation files, which split, which feature set, which seed) was as much engineering as the modeling.

**Retrain → predict.** New simulations land in object storage → a job re-derives features → triggers an evaluation sweep → if a candidate beats the live model on the held-out test, it's promoted. Strict dataset-partition discipline so no simulation ever crosses train/test through any path.

## What broke

The initial heading regressor looked spectacular on a stratified random split and fell apart on a temporal split. The model was learning per-burst structure that didn't generalise across simulation campaigns. Switching to campaign-level splits (a campaign goes entirely into either train or test, never both) cost a few R² points but produced numbers that survived contact with new data. That was the highest-impact change in the whole project.

## What I'd do differently

Add monotonicity constraints earlier on the wave-height head. They would have made the failure modes much more interpretable when domain experts pushed back on edge predictions.
