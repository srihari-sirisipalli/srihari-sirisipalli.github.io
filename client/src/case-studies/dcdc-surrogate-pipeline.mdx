## What it is

A machine-learning surrogate for a dual-input DC-DC power converter. The classical solver is accurate but slow. The surrogate predicts the same behaviour in microseconds so an engineer can actually explore the design space, or specify a target and get a candidate design back.

## Why it matters

Sweeping the converter's design space with the analytical solver is painful. Inverse design, where you fix the outcome and ask what components deliver it, is worse. The surrogate turns both into interactive work.

## What I built

**A predictive model.** Trained on samples from the analytical solver, so ground truth is definitionally correct. XGBoost held the strongest test performance among the models I tried; a physics-informed MLP came very close and generalises better at the edges.

**A physics-informed loss.** The relative gain array should satisfy a row-sum identity. Adding that as a soft constraint kept the network from drifting into physically impossible territory in the undersampled corners of the input space.

**An out-of-distribution gate.** The surrogate is a fast approximator, not an oracle. Predictions pass through a small stack of trust checks: Mahalanobis distance in the input space, ensemble variance across model heads, a physics residual on the RGA identity, bounds on each input, and a consistency check between predicted and reconstructed outputs. Fail two, and the prediction is flagged.

**Inverse design.** Differential evolution over the surrogate. You specify a target output vector, you get a candidate parameter set back in a handful of seconds. A small PyQt GUI wraps it for engineers who want to pin some parameters and search over the rest.

## What I learned

The first model looked great on average error and quietly violated the physics. Aggregate metrics can hide a lot. Once the physical invariant went into the loss, the failures stopped. Physical constraints belong in the training signal, not in the post-hoc audit.
