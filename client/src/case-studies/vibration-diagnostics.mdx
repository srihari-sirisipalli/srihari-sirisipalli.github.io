## The problem

Rotating machinery (motors, pumps, turbines, gearboxes) fails in a small number of characteristic ways: unbalance, misalignment, looseness, bearing defects, rotor bar issues, stator eccentricity. Each fault leaves a fingerprint in the vibration spectrum. Practitioners know these fingerprints well. What they need is a diagnostic tool that surfaces the right diagnosis with the right evidence, not a black-box classifier that just returns a label.

## The approach

A rule engine, not a classifier. Every diagnosis comes with a for-and-against evidence chain, so a maintenance engineer can read the reasoning and not just the verdict. Thirteen rules covering the standard fault families. Physically motivated features feed the rules; the rules produce ranked hypotheses; each hypothesis carries the specific evidence that supported it.

Bearing defect frequencies (BPFO, BPFI, BSF, FTF) are computed from bearing geometry. Overall vibration velocity is mapped to ISO 10816 severity zones (A/B/C/D). Each diagnosis carries a confidence score and a severity score. Together they answer both "what is going on" and "how urgent is it."

## Why rules, not ML

An unsupervised ML detector is good at flagging that something is different from baseline. A rule engine is good at saying what the difference means. For fault diagnosis in rotating machinery, where practitioners already know the fingerprints, transparency is more valuable than adaptivity. The ML counterpart to this tool is a separate project ([MFDMS](/work/machine-fault-detection-ml)) that handles the "different from baseline" half.

## What broke

The first BPFO detection worked well on textbook cases and missed real bearings whose defect frequency landed inside a strong harmonic sideband. Adding narrow-band envelope demodulation ahead of the FFT gave the right signal the room it needed to show up. Lesson: the textbook signal processing pipeline sometimes has to be inverted for the real signal.
