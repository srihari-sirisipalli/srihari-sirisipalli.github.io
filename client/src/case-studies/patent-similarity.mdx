## The problem

Patent prior-art search at scale is two coupled problems. You need semantically meaningful similarity between long, jargon-heavy documents. And you need to evaluate thousands of pairs per day without paying enterprise-grade infrastructure rates.

The original system at Pangeon ran the embedding and scoring stack on always-on EC2 instances. Throughput was bounded by node count, costs were bounded by node uptime, and both were higher than they should have been for a workload that is bursty by nature.

## Re-architecting onto Lambda

The dominant cost on EC2 was idle capacity. Instances sized for peak burst sat near-empty most of the time. Re-shaping the workload as a coordinator Lambda with a fan-out of scoring Lambdas matched the bursty traffic pattern directly.

Cold starts for the embedding container were the obvious risk. Pre-warmed concurrency on the coordinator path solved the user-perceived latency without paying for permanent capacity on the scoring path.

## Vector retrieval

FAISS index built over patent abstract embeddings, sharded by classification block. Top-k retrieval on the coordinator side narrows the candidate set before LLM scoring is paid for. The LLM is the unit-cost driver, so anything that reduces its call count compounds through the rest of the pipeline.

## Two levels of parallelism

Per-request, the scoring fan-out is async to AWS. Per-Lambda, the inner loop uses multiprocessing for batch scoring of the candidate window. Two independent levels of parallelism, both bounded by configuration, both visible in CloudWatch.

## Terraform as source of truth

Every client account gets the same stack via Terraform: Amplify front, Lambda compute, IAM, monitoring, security baseline. New environments are a `terraform apply` away, not a runbook. Reproducibility is the point.

## What broke

Initial Lambda concurrency limits clipped the scoring fan-out under burst. The queue grew faster than the function scaled. Catching it required a CloudWatch dashboard that tracked invocations against the concurrency cap, not just function errors. Once the cap was raised and a fall-through to async invocation was added, peak-day throughput stopped degrading.

The vector store also needed re-sharding twice as the corpus grew. Worth doing earlier next time.
